About

What you see, and how.

Every picture on this site is real SPHEREx data. Here is each step between the archive and your screen, what it cannot tell you, and whose work it rests on.

How we clean the data

  1. Find the exposures. We ask NASA IRSA's SPHEREx table (spherex.obscore) for every exposure that covers the position, in the four science collections spherex_qr2, spherex_qr2_deep, spherex_qr3 and spherex_qr3_deep. Calibration collections are skipped. Exposures taken close together in time form one visit.
  2. Cut out a small square. IRSA's cutout service returns about 3′ × 3′ around the target from each exposure, with all its layers: the image, the flags, the variance (noise), the zodiacal-light model and the PSF.
  3. Clean each exposure. Image minus the file's own zodiacal-light model. Noise σ = √variance. Bad pixels come from the official flag bits, read from each file's header because the two releases write them differently; the list of bad flags is in backend/app/config.py. The SOURCE flag is not treated as bad: it marks a known object, not a defect. Pixels with no value in the file (for example a saturated star core) stay empty.
  4. Line them up. Every exposure is reprojected (reproject_interp) onto one shared grid: north up, 6.15″ pixels, 29 × 29 pixels for 3′. Only then does "the same pixel" mean "the same place in the sky".
  5. Add up each visit. Up to 8 exposures per visit, closest to the visit's mean time (all of them, up to 21, for faint WISE 0855 at D6), weighted by 1 / σ² so noisier pixels count less. Flagged pixels are left out of the add-up. The flipbook label says how many were used.
  6. Show them fairly. All frames of one target share one brightness scale (1st to 99.5th percentile of the good pixels, or an asinh stretch for very bright stars), so a change in the picture is a change in the sky, not in the display.
  7. Difference. z = (B − A − sky change) / √(σA² + σB²), where the sky change is the median of B − A. Groups of pixels beyond 5σ are listed as spots, each with the reasons it may be an artifact.
  8. Check against Gaia. Gaia DR3 positions (epoch 2016.0) are moved to each visit's date with their proper motion. The proof panel compares the target's measured shift with Gaia's prediction.
  9. Light curve. Brightness inside a 2-pixel radius minus the median sky 10–14 pixels away, for each exposure. Visits are only compared at the same wavelength, because each exposure sees a slightly different colour.

The code for each step is in backend/app/: archive.py, clean.py, align.py, coadd.py, render.py, diff.py, spots.py, gaia.py, proof.py, lightcurve.py.

What it cannot tell you

  • Pixels are big. One SPHEREx pixel is 6.15″, and its PSF is about one pixel wide. A shift smaller than about one pixel is not reliable by eye; our own measurements are rough (pixel phase).
  • The data are young. Public SPHEREx data so far cover about April 2025 to October 2026, so most targets have only a few visits, about six months apart.
  • Colour changes look like brightness changes. Each exposure sees a slightly different wavelength, so two visits can differ in brightness for that reason alone.
  • Gaia markers skip parallax. They use proper motion only. For nearby stars the yearly parallax wobble can shift them a little.
  • A spot is not a discovery. Most come from moving or variable stars, artifacts, or noise. When a pair of visits scatters more than the noise files predict, the site says so.
  • The known-object check is limited. It asks NASA JPL's Small-Body Identification service. On 31 Oct 2026 JPL's accurate second pass failed for every request, so the site uses the rougher first pass and labels it. JPL's offsets are east- and north-positive (we checked with the asteroid Ceres and a box moved off it on purpose). "No match" never means "new object".
  • Our choices are marked as ours. Some limits come from our plan (0.5 px for motion), others are our own choices (30° in direction, 1.5 px for a nearby Gaia star, 2 px for a known-object match). The panels say which is which.
  • Prepared targets. The five official targets have ready-made frames for detector D2; Eps Ind B and RW Aur also for D1–D6, WISE 0855 also for D5 and D6. Other positions and detectors are made on request and need the data API.

Could this find a Planet X?

Probably not, and here is why, from geometry alone. A planet far beyond Neptune barely moves along its own orbit, but SPHEREx watches it from Earth, which goes round the Sun every year. That makes a distant planet seem to swing back and forth across the sky, more the closer it is.

    How these are worked out: Earth's move along its orbit (a chord of a 1 AU circle) divided by the distance, plus a circular orbit with Kepler's third law (period = distance1.5 years). "Up to" because the shift is largest when we look at right angles to Earth's motion. One SPHEREx pixel is 6.15″. Brightness: a planet the size and albedo of Neptune (about V 7.8 at 30 AU), with reflected sunlight falling as 1/(distance from the Sun × distance from Earth)². A cold planet's own heat glows at about 70 µm, far beyond SPHEREx's 5 µm.

    So a Planet X would not stay put in a 3′ flipbook square between visits: it would be in one visit and missing from the next. The way to look for one is a source that is present in only one visit and shifts a little between the exposures of that visit. The spot list points to the first clue, its "what would confirm it" notes ask for the second, and the known-object check rules out asteroids and comets that are already known. A spot is still never called a new object.

    Methane vision: how it works

    Brown dwarfs are too small to shine like stars and slowly cool. Below about 1300 K their air holds methane, which swallows light around 3.3 µm, while 3.9–4.2 µm stays clear and they shine there. Backyard Worlds volunteers spot such objects as orange movers in WISE's two broad colours; SPHEREx's narrow wavelengths see the methane band itself.

    1. Exposures chosen by wavelength. SPHEREx's filter gives every exposure its own wavelength at the target. Every D4 and D5 image of the position is downloaded from IRSA, and only those that saw the target at 3.15–3.50 µm (methane band) or 3.90–4.20 µm (window) are kept. The flipbook's own frames are not changed.
    2. A normal star made flat. Each exposure is multiplied by (wavelength / 4 µm)², so a normal star (Rayleigh–Jeans, Fν ∝ λ−2) is equally bright in both windows. The field's own stars then check this: k is the median of their 4 µm / 3.3 µm ratios when at least three are bright enough, and 1 otherwise.
    3. The subtraction. Per visit, flagged pixels left out: (4 µm − k × 3.3 µm) / noise. The noise includes 5% of each star's predicted light, because a star's image is slightly different in the two detectors and never cancels perfectly.
    4. A null test. The same measurement is repeated at every other pixel at least 10 pixels from the target (closer, its own faint wings would count). Neighbouring pixels share noise once the images are lined up, so it scatters more than the noise maps say; every significance is divided by that scatter (at least 1).
    5. The verdict. The target glows if its 4 µm light is at least twice what a normal star with its 3.3 µm light would have and the difference is at least 5σ; it has a normal colour within 3σ. A target with no value in its core in most exposures (saturated) is not tested. The proof panel's motion test is added: moving and glowing is the signature of a nearby cold brown dwarf.
    • A colour is not an identification. Dusty young stars, red galaxies and a star blended with a brown dwarf can glow too. A glow is a reason to look closer, and nothing is called a discovery.
    • Not simultaneous. D4 and D5 see different patches of sky in one exposure, so the two windows of a visit are hours to days apart. A brown dwarf that changes brightness in that time changes its colour here too.

    Code: backend/app/methane.py; tests in backend/tests/test_methane.py check that a fake normal star fades and a fake methane dwarf glows.

    Eclipse mode: how it works

    An eclipsing binary is two stars orbiting so that, seen from Earth, one passes in front of the other once per orbit. NASA's TESS timed hundreds of them near the north ecliptic pole, the centre of the SPHEREx North deep field, where SPHEREx looks thousands of times. Each SPHEREx exposure sees one wavelength at the star, so a single exposure can't show a colour picture of an eclipse. Thousands of them, sorted by orbit, can.

    1. The clock. TESS-EBs (Prša et al. 2022) lists each binary's period P and a time of primary eclipse t₀ in BTJD (BJDTDB − 2457000). SPHEREx times are UTC (MJD-AVG, the middle of the exposure), so each is converted to the solar-system barycentre with astropy: UTC to TDB, plus the light-travel time for the star's direction. Phase φ = frac((BTJD − t₀)/P − prim_pos), shown from −0.5 to 0.5 with the primary eclipse at 0. How far TESS's clock has drifted by 2026 is (N σP + σt₀)/P; binaries are shown only if it is below 1% of an orbit.
    2. Before downloading. The archive's exposure times alone say how many exposures fall in each eclipse (the coverage numbers on the page). Then a fixed number per detector is downloaded: in the primary eclipse, in the secondary and outside, each spread evenly in phase.
    3. Brightness. As for the light curve: light inside 2 pixels minus the sky 10–14 pixels away, on the same north-up grid. Any exposure with a flagged pixel in the circle is drawn hollow and not used.
    4. Normal brightness at every colour. A smooth curve S(λ) (cubic B-spline in ln λ, knots every two resolution elements) through the exposures taken outside both eclipses, separately per detector. Each exposure is then divided by S at its own wavelength, so 1 means "normal" whatever the colour.
    5. Depth. In each of two wavelength bins per detector: 1 − the weighted mean of the middle half of the eclipse. Short-period binaries also brighten and fade between eclipses (stretched stars, reflected light); a cosine baseline handles that when it is significant. Errors come from the noise maps plus a "jitter" chosen so the out-of-eclipse exposures scatter as much as their errors say.
    6. Six checks. Quarter-phase exposures (held out of the fit) must show no eclipse; comparison stars in the same pictures, folded on the binary's clock, must show none; in-eclipse minus out-of-eclipse pictures, paired by wavelength, must be darkest on the binary; SPHEREx's depth at 0.75–1.09 µm should be close to TESS's at 0.6–1.0 µm; a period search on SPHEREx data alone should find TESS's period; and the middle of the eclipse, fitted in SPHEREx data, should fall where TESS's clock says (a shift would mean the period is changing).
    • What it cannot tell you. These are known binaries: the eclipses are TESS's discovery. What SPHEREx adds is how deep each eclipse is from 0.75 to 5 µm, which says how the two stars' colours (temperatures) differ. The secondary-to-primary depth ratio is the ratio of the two stars' surface brightness and is not changed by light from other stars; single depths are, and TESS's 21″ pixels and SPHEREx's 6.15″ pixels take in different neighbours.
    • Exposures are not simultaneous. Each wavelength was seen on a different orbit. If the stars have spots or the orbit changes over the 1.5 years, the depth at one wavelength can differ from another for that reason.

    Code: backend/app/eclipses.py (catalogue, time, phase, coverage), backend/app/eclipse_depth.py (the depth recipe), backend/app/eclipse_build.py (download, measurement, pictures, checks); tests in backend/tests/test_eclipse.py recover known depths from fake binaries.
